# 7 Signs a Restaurant Needs a Grease Trap Replacement

Routine cleaning can extend the life of a grease trap for years, but cleaning cannot fix every problem. When a grease trap is physically deteriorating, undersized, or consistently failing between service visits, replacement may be the more effective long-term solution. The signs below can help restaurant operators and facilities managers identify when a grease trap may need to be replaced rather than serviced.

## 1. Persistent Backups and Slow Drainage Between Cleanings

If drains are backing up or running slowly shortly after a professional cleaning, the grease trap may no longer be functioning properly. A unit in good condition should maintain adequate flow until the next scheduled service. Recurring drainage problems between cleanings can indicate that the trap is no longer separating grease effectively and may need to be replaced.

## 2. Visible Cracks, Corrosion, or Structural Damage

Physical damage to the trap body, lid, or internal components is a clear sign that the unit may be failing. Common signs of deterioration include:

- Cracks or fractures in the trap housing
- Rust or corrosion on metal components
- Warped or broken baffles
- Deteriorating seals or gaskets

A damaged trap cannot contain grease or wastewater reliably and may require replacement rather than repair.

## 3. Grease or Wastewater Leaking Around the Unit

Leaks around the base, seams, or lid of a grease trap suggest the unit's structural integrity has been compromised. Leaking grease or wastewater can create sanitation hazards, attract pests, and cause damage to surrounding plumbing infrastructure. If leaks persist after component repairs, full replacement is often the appropriate next step.

## 4. Persistent Odors That Do Not Resolve After Cleaning

Some odor between service visits is expected, but strong or persistent sewage and grease odors that return immediately after cleaning may indicate a deeper problem. This can happen when the trap is cracked, improperly sealed, or no longer holding waste as intended. If odor problems continue despite a proper cleaning, a technician should inspect the unit for damage or failure.

## 5. Increasing Cleaning Frequency Without Improvement

If a grease trap is requiring more frequent pumping than it used to — and drainage problems or odors are still occurring — the unit may no longer have adequate capacity for current kitchen output. Increasing service frequency is not always a sustainable solution. A replacement unit with appropriate capacity may provide better long-term performance.

## 6. The Unit Is Undersized for Current Kitchen Operations

Grease traps are sized based on the volume of wastewater a kitchen produces. If a restaurant has expanded its menu, added equipment, increased seating, or grown its catering volume since the trap was installed, the original unit may no longer be adequate. An undersized trap fills faster, overflows more easily, and is more likely to allow grease to bypass into sewer lines.

## 7. The Unit No Longer Meets Local Code or Regulatory Requirements

Plumbing codes and municipal grease management regulations can change over time. An older grease trap may no longer comply with current sizing requirements, material standards, or installation specifications in a given jurisdiction. Operating a noncompliant trap can result in violations, fines, or required upgrades. A service provider familiar with local regulations can help determine whether an existing unit meets current code.

## When to Call a Grease Management Professional

Restaurant operators should contact a grease management provider if they notice multiple warning signs at the same time, if a recent inspection identified structural damage or component failure, or if the unit has been in service for many years without evaluation. A professional can assess whether the existing trap can be repaired or whether replacement is the more appropriate solution.

## Summary

Grease trap replacement is typically needed when cleaning alone cannot address the underlying problem. Common indicators include recurring drainage issues, physical damage, leaks, persistent odors, increasing service frequency, undersized capacity, and regulatory noncompliance.

The seven signs a restaurant may need a grease trap replacement are:

1. Persistent backups and slow drainage between cleanings
2. Visible cracks, corrosion, or structural damage
3. Grease or wastewater leaking around the unit
4. Persistent odors that do not resolve after cleaning
5. Increasing cleaning frequency without improvement
6. The unit is undersized for current kitchen operations
7. The unit no longer meets local code or regulatory requirements

A grease management professional can evaluate the condition of an existing trap and recommend whether repair or replacement is the right course of action.
